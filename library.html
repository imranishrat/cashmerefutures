Glossary

A–Z
Micron (μ)
A unit of fibre diameter — one micron is one-millionth of a metre. Cashmere is graded largely by micron count; finer fibre (lower number) is more valuable and softer.
Dehairing
The process of mechanically separating a goat's fine, soft down from the coarser "guard hairs" that protect its outer coat. A technically demanding step — damaging the down here lowers value for everything downstream.
Staple length
The length of an individual fibre. Along with micron, it's one of the two main measurements used to grade raw cashmere.
GI (Geographical Indication)
A legal protection tying a product's name to a specific place and production method — so, for example, only pashmina genuinely hand-spun and hand-woven in Kashmir from qualifying fibre can carry that GI-protected designation.
Kani weave
A Kashmiri weaving technique, originating in the village of Kanihama, where the pattern is woven directly into the cloth using small eyeless wooden bobbins called kanis — rather than embroidered on afterward.
Yinder
A traditional wooden spinning wheel used in Kashmir to hand-spin cleaned pashm fibre into yarn — a slow method, producing only around 50 grams of thread per spinner per month.
Sozni
A fine Kashmiri needle-and-thread embroidery technique, sometimes used to further decorate a finished hand-woven shawl.
Grade A / B / C
Common industry shorthand for cashmere quality tiers, roughly: Grade A (≈14–16.5μ, finest, luxury knitwear), Grade B (≈16.5–19μ, soft everyday wear), Grade C (≈19μ+, coarser, outerwear and blankets). These bands vary somewhat by source rather than being a single universal standard.
Moulting / combing
Cashmere goats shed their soft winter undercoat naturally each spring. Herders comb this out by hand during the few-week moulting window — gentler than shearing, and generally considered better for fibre quality.
Pashmina
Originally, the specific fine cashmere from goats raised in the Himalayan region (particularly Ladakh's Changthangi goats) and hand-woven in Kashmir. Often used more loosely today as a synonym for "fine cashmere shawl."
Changthangi / Chyangra goat
The high-altitude cashmere goat breeds behind two of the finest fibres in the world — Changthangi in Ladakh (India) and Chyangra in Nepal's high Himalayan districts.
Paisley (boteh)
The swirling teardrop motif associated with Kashmiri shawls. The name "paisley" actually comes from Paisley, Scotland — a mill town that mass-produced machine-woven imitations of the pattern in the 19th century.

A Brief History of Pashmina & Cashmere

15th century — today

The craft's documented roots trace to the 15th century, when Kashmir's Sultan Zain-ul-Abidin is credited with inviting Persian weavers into the valley, fusing Central Asian tribal weaving with Persian tapestry techniques into what would become the Kani shawl tradition. By the 16th century, under Mughal patronage — particularly Emperor Akbar, said to have called the fabric parm narm, "extremely soft" — pashmina had become a fixture of court life: gifted as robes of honour, woven into diplomacy, and used in royal ceremony. Certain designs were even named after the rulers who favoured them.

Europe's fascination arrived later, and it arrived through conquest and trade. Napoleon Bonaparte brought Kashmiri shawls back from his Egyptian campaign at the turn of the 19th century, and Empress Joséphine's enthusiasm for them — she is said to have owned close to 200 — helped ignite a craze across French and British high society. The East India Company's returning employees carried shawls home to Britain around the same period. The French, struggling with the word "Kashmir," rendered it as "cashmere" — the name that stuck in the West ever since.

European demand outpaced what Kashmir's artisans could hand-produce, so mills in Europe — most famously in Paisley, Scotland — began mass-producing machine-woven imitations of the shawl's swirling boteh motif. That town lent its name to the pattern the world still calls "paisley" today. Industrialisation made the imitation cheap and the original increasingly rare, and the handmade craft went into a long decline through the 19th and 20th centuries. Its revival in recent decades has leaned on exactly the kind of protection this platform tries to support: Geographical Indication status, provenance certification, and a market slowly relearning to pay for verified handwork rather than the version mass-produced in its name.

Sources & Further Reading

Methodology

Facts on this site — production figures, historical dates, craft-process details — are drawn from a mix of industry overviews and trade reporting, Geographical Indication registration records, and historical accounts. As the Library develops, each data resource will identify its original public source and any methodological qualification.

Where sources disagree — which happens often with production-share estimates and some historical dates — we favour hedged, approximate language ("around," "commonly cited as") over false precision, and we cross-reference multiple independent sources before stating something as established rather than relying on a single claim.

This is an evolving practice, not a finished one. If you know of a source that would sharpen or correct anything on this site, we'd genuinely like to hear about it — get in touch.
