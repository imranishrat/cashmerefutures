Communities

Cashmere communities at the centre.

Exploring livelihoods, wellbeing, inclusion, resilience and opportunities for the communities whose lives are connected to cashmere — from herding households to artisans and cooperatives.

The people behind the fibre rarely appear in the conversation about it.

Cashmere connects two distinct communities: herding households — often in remote, high-altitude, or arid regions — who raise the goats, and artisan communities of spinners and weavers who turn raw fibre into finished cloth. Both depend on the sector for their livelihoods, and both are frequently the last to be consulted about decisions that affect them.

This section is where we bring their perspective forward — not as a footnote to industry or research, but as the starting point.

In Kashmir, a shawl can carry 200 hours of a single artisan's hands.

The finest pashmina begins with the Changthangi goat, herded at altitude across Ladakh's high plateau, where fibre as fine as 12–16 microns grows to survive the cold. From there, Kashmir's artisan communities — centred historically in and around Srinagar — take over: spinners, historically and often still women working from home, draw the cleaned down into yarn entirely by hand, on a traditional wooden wheel called a yinder, producing only around 50 grams of thread in a month. Weavers then set that yarn on a wooden handloom, where a single shawl can take 180 to 250 hours to complete — and an especially intricate piece considerably longer. Some patterns, like the Kani weave from the village of Kanihama, are woven directly into the cloth using small eyeless wooden bobbins called kanis, building a design entirely on the loom rather than embroidering it on afterward; a single richly patterned Kani shawl has historically taken as long as two years to complete. Finished pieces are sometimes further decorated with sozni, a fine needle-and-thread embroidery worked entirely by hand.

Genuine hand-spun, hand-woven pashmina is famous for a simple test: a shawl fine enough can be drawn through a finger ring. This entire process — hand-spun and hand-woven, from a specific goat, in a specific place — carries Geographical Indication (GI) protection in India. But it's also under real pressure: industrial spinning is faster and cheaper, and much of what's sold globally as "pashmina" today is no longer hand-made at all. Supporting this craft isn't nostalgia — it's one of the more direct ways the sector can keep a centuries-old livelihood viable.

A brief history — from Persian looms to Napoleon's Paris.

The craft's documented roots trace to the 15th century, when Kashmir's Sultan Zain-ul-Abidin is credited with inviting Persian weavers into the valley, fusing Central Asian tribal weaving with Persian tapestry techniques into what would become the Kani shawl tradition. By the 16th century, under Mughal patronage — particularly Emperor Akbar, said to have called the fabric parm narm, "extremely soft" — pashmina had become a fixture of court life: gifted as robes of honour, woven into diplomacy, and used in royal ceremony. Certain designs were even named after the rulers who favoured them.

Europe's fascination arrived later, and it arrived through conquest and trade. Napoleon Bonaparte brought Kashmiri shawls back from his Egyptian campaign at the turn of the 19th century, and Empress Joséphine's enthusiasm for them — she is said to have owned close to 200 — helped ignite a craze across French and British high society. The East India Company's returning employees carried shawls home to Britain around the same period. The French, struggling with the word "Kashmir," rendered it as "cashmere" — the name that stuck in the West ever since.

European demand outpaced what Kashmir's artisans could hand-produce, so mills in Europe — most famously in Paisley, Scotland — began mass-producing machine-woven imitations of the shawl's swirling boteh motif. That town lent its name to the pattern the world still calls "paisley" today. Industrialisation made the imitation cheap and the original increasingly rare, and the handmade craft went into a long decline through the 19th and 20th centuries. Its revival in recent decades has leaned on exactly the kind of protection this platform tries to support: Geographical Indication status, provenance certification, and a market slowly relearning to pay for verified handwork rather than the version mass-produced in its name.
